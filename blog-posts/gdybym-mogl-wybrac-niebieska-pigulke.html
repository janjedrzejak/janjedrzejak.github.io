If I Could Take the Blue Pill

2026-11-01JAN JĘDRZEJAK

I sometimes wonder what the world would look like today if nothing remarkable had happened at the end of November 2022. ChatGPT would never have appeared. Large language models would have remained a subject for a relatively small group of researchers, and artificial intelligence would still have felt remote to most of us, hidden somewhere inside search engines and information systems.

Probably not much would look different. We would still use the internet, shop on our phones, watch films on streaming platforms and complain about the number of messages in our inboxes. The difference is that we would have to answer most of those messages ourselves. A programmer might spend longer looking for a bug, a student would read a few more articles, and someone preparing a presentation might spend an evening on it rather than fifteen minutes.

It doesn't sound particularly appealing.

Yet something about the picture feels strangely peaceful. Perhaps that peace is imaginary, a product of our habit of romanticising the past. People were overworked, distracted and overwhelmed by information long before ChatGPT arrived. The internet had already taken a substantial share of our attention.

Still, whenever I remember the scene in The Matrix in which Morpheus offers Neo two pills, the choice seems a little less obvious than it once did.

If the blue one meant waking up tomorrow in a world where generative AI had never entered everyday life, would it really be so easy to turn it down?

It is hard not to recognise the value of this technology when you work with it. Thoughtful automation can spare people hours of repetitive work. Predictive systems can reveal problems before they become costly. Generative AI helps people pursue ideas that once required skills, time or money they simply didn't have.

There is no reason to pretend these gains are small. AI also does work far more important than drafting messages or generating code. In science and medicine it helps tackle problems that conventional approaches have struggled to solve.

It would be difficult to make a convincing case that the world ought to give up those possibilities willingly.

When More Starts to Mean Faster

And yet the widespread availability of language models has changed more than the way we work. It has changed what we expect work to look like.

If a report used to take a day and now takes an hour, there is room to complete several more tasks. Before long, all of them may become the new standard. What first looked like an extraordinary improvement quickly stops attracting attention.

Not long ago, generating a working program from a plain-language description sounded almost fantastic. Today we get irritated when a model fails to understand the request on its first attempt.

Extraordinary things become ordinary remarkably fast.

Technology has promised to save us time for decades. Each new tool is supposed to help us work less, work better and live more comfortably. Yet there is little sign that everyone has more free time as these capabilities grow.

Of course we cannot blame technology alone. People set deadlines, organise work and decide what they expect of one another. AI can reduce the workload just as easily as it can provide an excuse to increase it.

What troubles me is how readily we accept that if something can be done faster, then it should be done faster.

Eventually that attitude escapes the workplace. It begins to shape everyday tasks, answers, decisions, learning and creativity. We even try to plan our rest so that it will be as efficient as possible.

At some point it becomes difficult to separate a genuine need from the habit of constant acceleration.

The Road We Can Skip

There is another issue, perhaps a less obvious one.

For years, starting work on a difficult problem meant accepting that we would not know the answer for a while. We had to search for information, read documentation and try different approaches. Sometimes an entire afternoon went into a problem that ultimately came down to a single line of code.

By the standards of efficiency, that afternoon was wasted.

Not necessarily by the standards of the person trying to understand the problem.

Not every difficulty is valuable, of course. Hours spent tracking down a bug that could have been detected automatically do not make anyone a better specialist. Frustration does not always lead to discovery. Sometimes it is just frustration.

But there is a kind of effort in which understanding grows slowly. A mistaken assumption leads to another question, which leads to another, until the answer turns out to be quite different from what we first expected.

Today we can increasingly skip that stage.

We receive a solution, check it and sometimes adjust it. The task is finished, but the understanding we might have gained by finding our own way to the answer does not necessarily come with the result.

We do not yet know the long-term consequences of this change. Research into generative AI and critical thinking is only beginning to examine relationships that are still taking shape. It would be dishonest to declare that artificial intelligence is already robbing people of the ability to think.

But it would be equally hasty to assume that getting good answers faster automatically makes us wiser.

In his famous thought experiment about the experience machine, Robert Nozick asked whether someone would agree to spend an entire life connected to a device that could provide any desired experience. Every success, pleasure or fulfilled dream would feel real, although none of it would actually happen.

Nozick suggested that many people would refuse. We care about more than the feeling of having accomplished something. We also want to have genuinely done it.

AI is not Nozick's machine. A program written with its help may work just as well as one written alone. A report does not become useless because an algorithm performed part of the analysis.

Still, somewhere between the outcome and the way we reach it lies something that cannot easily be measured in productivity percentages.

The satisfaction of solving a problem ourselves. Understanding a subject in our own terms. The feeling that an idea, however imperfect, really belongs to us.

Similar questions emerge in creative work.

A few sentences are now enough to produce a reasonably good article, story or design. We can ask for a change in tone, more emotion or stronger arguments. Another version, then another, until everything sounds exactly as it should.

But after reading dozens of these texts, it is difficult to escape the feeling that many of them sound suspiciously alike.

They are all correct. Every paragraph leads neatly to the next. The reflection arrives on cue, followed by an impressive question and a final line meant to leave the reader with something important.

Over time, that predictability becomes tiring.

A study by Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser, published in Science Advances in 2024, found an interesting tension. AI-generated ideas improved the assessed creativity of individual short stories, but made the stories more similar to one another.

Sometimes a slightly awkward text is far more interesting, written by someone who apparently did not worry about making every sentence sound perfect. It follows unfamiliar associations, loses its thread occasionally and returns to an idea that seemed finished. We may disagree with it, but at least we can sense somebody's particular view of the world behind the words.

There is a certain irony in an age when everyone can write more fluently, yet finding something we genuinely want to read can feel harder.

That is not a case for deliberately writing badly. It is a reason not to confuse polished form with worthwhile content.

The same convenience is beginning to enter more personal parts of life.

We can talk to AI about problems, decisions and doubts. It is available at any hour and never needs to end the conversation. For many people this is real help, particularly when there is no one around who can listen without judging.

But another person is not a well-designed interface. People get tired, misunderstand us and disagree. Sometimes they respond in ways we could never have predicted. It is hard to know how widespread conversations with AI will affect relationships, but closeness with another person also involves accepting these uncomfortable moments.

Not all the costs of technology are psychological. Behind a convenient chat window are data centres, energy consumption, enormous investments and an increasing concentration of technological power. There are questions about privacy and the future of work. If machines take over many of the simpler tasks through which younger workers once learned their professions, we will need other ways to build experience.

Technology has always brought change. This time, though, new capabilities are entering everyday life at a pace that leaves unusually little room to consider their consequences.

And perhaps this rush is the most unsettling part of the story.

What Is the Point of Having a Choice?

It seems unfair to resent a tool for working too well. If a task can be finished faster, an answer found more easily or pointless work avoided, why shouldn't we use it?

The problem begins when an opportunity slowly turns into an expectation.

When every hour saved becomes another hour to fill. When finding a solution independently starts to look like an unnecessary waste of time. When a delay in receiving an answer causes impatience, even though a few years ago nobody would have found it remarkable.

Perhaps the imagined world without generative AI is tempting because some of these new expectations would not exist there.

That does not mean life would be better. Many things would probably be harder, and some valuable ideas would never be realised. It is easy to long for the past when we do not have to experience its limitations again.

Nor can we expect everyone to constantly weigh every consequence of the tools they use. Most of us simply want to do our work, solve a problem and move on to something else.

Still, it would be good to retain the ability to choose.

Not every message has to be written in seconds. Not every idea has to be developed immediately. We do not have to seize every opportunity to increase efficiency, just as we do not have to do everything ourselves to prove that we are capable.

Tools ought to give us more freedom, rather than making our list of obligations longer with every new capability.

That is why I still do not know whether I would take the blue pill.

I would probably start missing the things AI has made easier almost immediately. The convenience, the possibilities, the ideas that would require far more work without it. It is difficult to imagine knowingly giving up a technology whose value is so visible in practice.

But another thought remains.

If the prospect of a world where some things took more effort and time can feel reassuring, perhaps this is not simply nostalgia.

Perhaps it is a sign that amid all this acceleration we have lost some room for idleness, reflection and doing things without having to justify them as the best possible use of time.

I do not know whether a world without generative AI would be a better one.

What puzzles me more is why a world with fewer possibilities has begun to sound like a promise of rest.
